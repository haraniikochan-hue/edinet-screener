### Inferences
- The committee's reaction function is asymmetric right now: the bar for a December hike is "no improvement in the inflation data," while the bar for a cut is a clear labor-market break. Warsh's "information-rich interval" refers to the Oct 29 GDP/PCE, the Nov 6 and Dec 4 jobs reports, the Nov 10 CPI, and the Nov 25 October PCE — all of which land before the Dec 8–9 meeting (November CPI does not).
- Two hike dissents from a governor plus a president, the Sept dots (one more 2026 hike), and Waller's "not necessarily consecutive" framing together imply the October hold was a timing choice (pre-midterm, waiting for Q3 data) rather than a pivot. The default for December is a hike unless data soften.

### Inferences
- Pass-through is still building, not fading: the three-month supercore run-rate (~4.5% annualized), the largest core-goods monthly gain since Jan 2025, apparel at 3.4% y/y, the Beige Book's "eleventh consecutive period" of input-cost pressure, and ISM prices at 77.9 all point the same way. The July 24, 2026 switch from the Section 122 surcharge to Section 301 "forced labor" tariffs (10%/12.5%) resets the pass-through clock for a new tariff layer.
- The core PCE > core CPI inversion (3.0% vs 2.6%) means the Fed's preferred gauge looks worse than CPI; the BEA methodology change already took ~30bp off core PCE, so the September print at ~3.1% would be consistent with the SEP's 3.4% year-end path only if monthly prints stay near 0.3%.
- A measurable energy pass-through into services (airfares +22% y/y, hotels +2% m/m) makes "supercore" an imperfect gauge of underlying demand pressure; the Fed will likely look through gasoline but not through broadening services.

## 7. What the Fed will look at in each release, and what would tip the December decision

### Takeaway
Given the hike bias, the December decision hinges on whether the October CPI (Nov 10) and October core PCE (Nov 25) confirm a ~0.3%/month core run-rate (hike) or fall back to ≤0.2% with supercore cooling (hold), and whether the Nov 6 / Dec 4 jobs reports keep unemployment at ≤4.2% with claims <220k (hike-compatible) or show unemployment ≥4.4% with continuing claims rising (hold, and the first conditions under which cut talk returns).

### Inferences (release-by-release, thresholds are the researcher's judgment anchored on the cited facts)
- Oct 29 Q3 GDP advance + September PCE: the Fed will look at core PCE m/m (consensus 0.3%; a 0.2% print with 3.0% y/y would be a dovish surprise), real PCE growth and the saving rate (to gauge whether gasoline is crowding out real spending), and GDP vs the ~3.2–3.3% consensus. Hike-compatible: core PCE ≥0.3% and GDP ≥3%. Hold-compatible: core PCE ≤0.2% and real consumption decelerating.
- Nov 6 October jobs report: unemployment rate first (4.2% stable = hike-compatible; 4.4%+ = hold; a move toward 4.5% would reopen the easing debate), then payrolls judged against a 15–87k breakeven (so anything ≥50k is "solid"; a negative print with revisions would matter), household/establishment divergence after September's calendar quirk, prime-age EPOP (watch for the "spurious pop" Employ America flagged), AHE (3.0% y/y is non-inflationary), and revisions to September's 29k.
- Nov 10 October CPI: supercore m/m (≤0.25% needed for the hawks to pause; a fourth month ≥0.3% = hike), shelter/OER (0.3% vs the 0.35–0.4% re-acceleration), core goods (another ≥0.3% would confirm tariff pass-through is building), and headline energy (gasoline follows Brent ~$115, so headline stays ~3.7–3.8%). The Cleveland nowcast (0.21% core) sets a soft benchmark; a print at or below it argues for hold, above 0.3% argues for hike.
- Nov 13 October PPI: the PCE-relevant components (airfares, portfolio management/financial services, medical services) that drive the Oct 29 and Nov 25 PCE translations; airfares were "hot" in September.
- Nov 3 JOLTS (Sept): quits rate (1.9% = no wage pressure; a rise to 2.1%+ would signal worker leverage returning), layoffs rate (1.0%; >1.2% would be a deterioration signal), openings/unemployed ratio.
- Weekly claims (every Thursday): continuing claims is the series to watch; initial claims <200k and continuing <1.75M support hiking; a sustained move in initial claims above ~230–240k with continuing >1.85M would be the first hard evidence against a hike.
- Nov 25 October PCE + Q3 second estimate: core PCE m/m vs the ~0.33% pace implied by the SEP; a 0.2% print following a soft October CPI would make December a genuine hold debate; 0.3%+ locks in the hike. Also the 3-month annualized core PCE (if ≥3.5% the hawks win; ≤2.5% the doves have a case).
- Dec 4 November jobs report: the last major input (plus any Dec 3–4 claims). Same hierarchy: unemployment rate > continuing claims > payrolls. Note the November reference week falls after the midterms and amid possible renewed Iran strikes.
- Expectations (NY Fed SCE Oct, UMich Nov prelim/final before the blackout): 5–10-yr UMich at 3.5% and NY Fed 3-yr at 3.3% are the "un-anchoring" argument the Cook/Hammack camp uses; any further rise strengthens the hike case regardless of realized prints.
- Summary thresholds for December: Hike (base case, ~85% priced) if October core CPI ≥0.3% or October core PCE ≥0.3%, unemployment ≤4.2%, claims <220k. Hold if both core prints ≤0.2% with supercore ≤0.25% and no further rise in expectations, or if unemployment ≥4.4%/continuing claims >1.85M. A cut is not plausible for December under any reasonable data path; cut talk would require unemployment ≥4.5% plus a drop in oil that pulls headline toward 3%.

## 8. Data distortions and measurement caveats to flag

### Takeaway
No shutdown is affecting the Oct–Dec 2026 data flow, but the backdrop contains several distortions: BEA's September 30 PCE methodology rewrite (cut July core PCE 3.3%→3.0%), a Labor Day calendar quirk and CPS rotation-group effects in the September jobs data, a −79k preliminary benchmark revision (final in Feb 2027), immigration-driven collapse in breakeven payroll growth, energy-shock leakage into "supercore" via airfares/transport, and a tariff regime that changed on July 24 (Section 122 → Section 301), resetting pass-through timing and distorting customs-revenue data via ~$130–145bn of refunds.
